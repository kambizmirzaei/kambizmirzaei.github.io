Musical Journey

A Placeless Harmony

The Musical Journey of Kambiz Mirzaei

Today I have lived half of my life in Canada. There is something unsettling about saying that aloud. At some point, without any ceremony, one place becomes where most of your life has happened.

It is a muggy summer evening, and I am standing somewhere along Lakeshore Boulevard, looking out across Lake Ontario while I wait for my ride. The city is behind me for a moment. Across the water, the Toronto Islands are fading into the evening light, ferries moving between the shore and the islands. I remember my first ride there decades ago—the smell of the air, the view of downtown from the islands, and the strange feeling that an entire world could be so close and still feel unexplored.

Then, for no particular reason after all these years, the thought arrives: Maybe I should have stayed in Iran. Maybe I should have stayed and made film music with Jamshid Andalibi.

It is an odd question to have after so long. Canada has been home long enough that, by any ordinary measure, there should be very little left to reconsider. Yet some roads remain visible even after you have stopped walking them.

I came to Canada in 1997. Music was not a career I was leaving behind, nor one I was planning to build. I simply did not know what place it would eventually have in my life.

What I wanted then was relatively simple: safety, independence, stability, and perhaps, in time, a chance to help my family in Iran join me.

Pharmacy seemed perfectly capable of giving me those things.

I had a different relationship with music. I didn’t want anything from it. I thought it would come into my life on its own, when the time was right.

Looking back, what I see is not a conventional musical career so much as a sequence of openings. One thing would appear in front of me; I would enter it, feel it, learn what I could, and eventually something else would catch my attention. The first of those openings came in Tehran, with a neighbour, a Ney, and a keyboard.

A Neighbour, a Ney, and a Keyboard

It was a few years after the Iran-Iraq War. I was out of university and almost finished with my five-year obligations for health-care services in remote areas and military service. Whenever I could, I took my Technics five-octave keyboard with me. I had it in Dezful, where I took lessons from Seroj Sarkisian during my weekend trips to Ahvaz, and later in Shadegan.

That keyboard was my first serious upgrade since I had begun trying to understand music as a child. I remember my first moment of fascination with it, standing on my toes at a house wedding, trying to follow the keyboard player’s hands all night. For many reasons, my serious musical discoveries came relatively late, in my early twenties. The reason for that could make another story. The war had ended a few years earlier. I remember that time because I had been sent to the front line in Kurdistan on a mandatory student assignment, trained as a paramedic, but the ceasefire meant I was released and sent back to university earlier than expected.

Much of the music that had been suppressed after the revolution had not returned, especially pop music. Looking back, it would never really return in the way it once was. Traditional Persian music occupied much of the public space that remained.

Jamshid Andalibi and his family were my parents’ neighbours and soon became family friends. I moved between my own apartment and my parents’ home.

The Ney was Jamshid’s universe. Mine was becoming increasingly occupied by Western music, pop, progressive and psychedelic rock, piano practice, electronic instruments and drum machines, the new generation of keyboard workstations, and the pleasure of figuring out what the technology of the day could do for music. Back then, that was the frontier.

Jamshid had a Korg 01/W, a workstation keyboard that contained a 16-track recorder inside a single instrument. He wanted to understand it. I liked figuring keyboards out, so he asked me to work it out for him.

Rather than explain every function, I put a few tracks together and showed him what I had discovered. I was not thinking about a musical career. I was simply trying to understand an instrument well enough to explain it to another musician through a simple piece of music I had put together.

Jamshid heard it. He asked, “Did you do all this?” I said, “Yes.” Then he asked whether, if he gave me a melody, I could build something around it.

I did not know.

I said I could try.

That was the conversation that changed everything. A piece of music I put together on his Korg 01/W became my true introduction to the world of professional musicians.

What came next was the Ney.

Breathing Different Air

At the time, traditional Persian music was not part of my listening diet. You could not escape it in Iran—it was in taxis, on television, on the radio—but I did not seek it out. My ears had been shaped much more by Western music.

So when Jamshid’s melodies arrived, I did not hear them as tradition asking to be preserved or modernized. I heard them as a puzzle.

What do I do with the sori and korons? What can live around this melody? What should remain untouched? How would a bass line wrap around it? How far could I move the arrangement before the Ney stopped sounding like itself? Which instrumental path should I choose for this production?

Almost everything I tried seemed to work seamlessly. Usually it was my own playing that needed adjustment, just enough to create the impression of a professional player.

The technical question gradually became a musical one: Could the Ney breathe in a different air without surrendering its identity?

We worked one melody at a time. I wrote two pieces independently for Ney. He liked them enough to include them, and the material gradually became Paeize Neyzar.

With Safar Rafteh, I wanted to move the Ney away from its familiar placement, toward something less folk-defined and more national. With Gerdaab, I went further. I wanted to throw the Ney into ‘world music’ rather than keep it within its familiar boundaries: electronic strings, an orchestral direction, guitar at a time when guitar was still banned. I was not trying to make a statement. I heard the combination in my head and wanted to hear it exist, and Jamshid agreed. For him, it was more about increasing the Ney’s reach.

This was new ground for Ney, so I did not expect much from the album. Two or three hundred listeners, perhaps. Lots of criticism. That seemed more than plausible for defining new boundaries for a Persian traditional instrument. “Many won’t like what we did conceptually,” I thought to myself. But I knew it had to exist.

In Pejvak Studio, working on the first track with Hassan Asgari, the mix engineer, who already had credits for mixing some prominent Persian artists’ tracks, we were finishing the placement of Mehrdad Arabi’s Zarb and Farzad Andalibi’s Daf on tape. I remember another guy in the studio saying out loud, “This music is out of this world.”

It stayed with me.

By the time Paeize Neyzar was published, I was already in Canada. I had left the work behind in Iran. Jamshid later told me what the response had been and that it had become his best-selling album, even though he did not get the budget required to record live strings and live piano instead of my demo lines. I had been thinking about making something that worked, not what would happen to it afterward. Once a piece of music leaves you, it develops a life you can no longer control.

And somewhere in that first chapter sits a road I did not take. Had I stayed in Iran, perhaps Jamshid and I would have continued working together. There were possibilities around film music. Jamshid was approached by some industry leaders of the time who wanted to work with us.

Perhaps I would have taken that road.

I didn’t.

Jamshid passed away in March 2024. The title “Paeize Neyzar” was used for his one-year memorial service in 2025 at Rudaki Hall.

Canada, Before I Knew What Music Was Becoming

When I came to Canada, music did not disappear. Before I received my pharmacy license in 1999, I was already living in a one-bedroom apartment on Redpath Street, an upgrade from the studio apartment where I had studied for my pharmacy licensing exams, trying to establish a life in a new country.

I had a computer, a broken guitar that travelled with me from Iran, some old poetry, a few new ideas, and enough curiosity to keep building. Music was still there, but it had no obvious place in my new life. I was not thinking about a career in it. I was simply looking for a creative outlet.

Eventually I put up a small website with some of my old poetry, thinking that, for the time being, this might be my only practical expressive channel. I did not know who would read it. I was not expecting anything to come from it.

Then I saw Shahrzad Sepanlou singing Bolvare Mirdamad one day. It was on a Persian TV channel.

What caught my attention was the fact that she cared about lyrical depth in a Persian pop song. That was refreshing. I remember thinking, and saying it aloud, that there was something in her artistic sensibility I could work with. People in the room heard me.

Then, unexpectedly, she contacted me.

The first email is dated September 24, 2002. She found me through the website I had built, looking for lyrics for her upcoming album.

At the time, I had no idea I was entering another musical chapter.

I wrote Baa To along with ten other lyrics in one night after she emailed me. I had never written lyrics before. I did not sit down and decide that I was now a lyricist. I had never done that with composing or arranging either. I wrote the lyrics because they needed to be written. I liked Baa To more than the others, felt it was coherent, made a few compromises where rhythm and lyrical form required them in the chorus, and sent it over.

She liked it.

The song was released in 2003.

Something shifted. My field of exploration.

I had found another language of music: lyrics, a continuation of the poetry I had been writing since I was a kid. The possibility of writing songs opened something different in me. I began thinking not only about how music could carry a feeling, but how words could shape it. A three-minute song carrying a few lines of lyrics could stay in the collective memory of a nation for years.

For a while, I followed that opening without knowing where it would lead. Most of the time, the lyrics came first. Harmony and melody followed together, although sometimes the order reversed itself. When somebody commissioned something, the process changed. An assignment created a direction. But when the work was mine, I wrote first and thought about singers later.

I did not have one vocalist who could sing all the lyrics and melodies I wrote, and that turned out to be useful. It allowed me to stay immersed in my creations without having to decide beforehand what the voice of an entire project should be.

Looking back, I hear longing everywhere. Sometimes the frustration of absence. Sometimes the fantasy of reunion. Sometimes a beloved who remains deliberately undefined. Maybe I never felt at home in any geography. In my lyrics, I am searching for where I truly belong.

I was not analyzing any of it while I wrote.

I wrote what I felt. Then I listened back and felt what I had written again.

I thought I had found my language. I did not yet know how many languages music was going to ask me to learn.

The next opening was Aadamaaye Kaaghazi.

The Wynford Apartment — Aadamaaye Kaaghazi

Between Redpath and the Wynford apartment, life entered.

A revolution, a war and an immigration can shape a person permanently, but life had more surprises for me at the personal level. This project became part of how I coped. I remember the apartment more than I remember the equipment: sitting there, listening to the songs grow, sometimes feeling more than I could contain. A new crowd, new relationships, a new chapter just starting. The music was helping me heal.

Aadamaaye Kaaghazi grew out of the Wynford condo apartment on the 29th floor beside large floor-to-ceiling windows. The city was my backyard. I had lost a year in my life. It was mixed with some reflections, feelings, and resolutions. A bit rushed, maybe. I was not myself for a while.

What had started as songs and lyrics on pieces of scattered papers and a computer was becoming something much larger. I began building a recording environment with help from my brother Kamyar, who had recently entered Canada with the rest of my family. I wanted to do serious recording for the first time. I learned by reading, listening, experimenting, making mistakes and trying to understand why something worked when it did. Then I started looking for singers—several singers—because one voice simply could not carry the variety of lyrics and melodies I wanted to make. This gave me more freedom of expression. I could stay inside the world of the songs without having to decide that one voice had to define the entire project.

The project took more than a year. The record was becoming a world of acoustic and electronic textures—live instrumentalists layered over programming, traditional Persian instruments beside Western ones. More than twenty singers eventually came through the apartment to audition. There was a good energy around the project. Many responded positively to the idea of raw, minimalist, experimental arrangements around deeper lyrical expression—an alternative to the Persian pop music of the day. I still have the audition sessions somewhere on my drive. They are part of my audio memory album.

Shahrzad sang two songs, both recorded at Silver Birch, since the album had already been moved there for mixing and mastering when she arrived in Toronto. Aadamaaye Kaaghazi kept me busier than ever, and that got me through some rough days.

Ghadre To was one of the simplest songs in the project:

برای نفس کشیدن هوای تو رو میخوام

I can only breathe in YOUR air

I hear those words differently now. They feel more nostalgic, more distant, more emotionally exposed than the person I have become.

I still hear things in that record that I like. It wandered away from mainstream pop. It had its own instincts. It was raw. It didn’t follow a commercial path by any means. I also hear what I would change. A mixer who cannot yet hear his own mistakes.

That made me want to understand mixing, mastering—everything related to sound, from start to finish.

Learning to Hear

The work eventually gave me a problem I could not solve simply by listening harder.

Before the final mixes were ready, I took the album to Los Angeles. I visited Avang and Taraneh Records and played it for people whose ears I trusted. The reaction was encouraging but clear: the record had something, but the mix could be improved.

I knew something was off, but I could not diagnose it.

That was an important realization. First you have to learn to hear the problem. But hearing a problem and understanding it are not the same thing.

I had not mixed Paeize Neyzar or Aadamaaye Kaaghazi. A sound engineer had done the mixing on both albums. My role had been production and arrangement, playing the keys, and some songwriting. Now I wanted to understand what was actually happening inside the sound, and no one could change my mind.

“But this is not your job,” they would say.

It was not about whose job it was. For me, it was a new technical puzzle that needed to be solved by me. It couldn’t be more difficult than learning biochemistry or pharmacology. For me, it was. I was wrong.

Those technical questions became part of my musical education—studios around town, discussions with mixing engineers, courses, and a whole lot of educational videos. The writing didn’t stop during this period. Az Man Taa Man, written for Shahrzad, came out as a single in 2006 and found its way onto her 2010 album Yek Rooz. But the larger preoccupation was sound itself—what lived inside it, what I was missing, and how to close that distance.

My serious mixing journey began in 2006. By Lahzehaa, written in 2012 and recorded in 2014, I felt I had moved beyond being an amateur mixer. Mastering took several more years.

I had not set out to become an engineer. I wanted to hear better. I wanted to understand the relationship between sounds and how I could manipulate them to move closer to the raw material I needed for my own artistic expression.

And somewhere in all of that, I was also a full-time clinical pharmacist, with on-call shifts after hours.

The Yonge and Finch Apartment — The Live Bands

After 2008, I moved into a new two-bedroom condo in the Yonge and Finch area. A bedroom studio, a Yamaha Tyros II for inspiration, and, at one point, an expectation that I would play keys in a developing band.

The drummer was a no-show.

I looked at the drum set sitting in the middle of the rehearsal space and curiosity hit. That was enough. I began learning drums—because it was there, and because that is usually reason enough for me.

It got serious. I eventually played with two jazz bands I put together myself, and for a period we were the house band at Coffee Jazz Mixers on Yonge and Finch. Jazz was a new language. I had come through rock drumming, which prepared me technically but not temperamentally for what jazz required—dynamic sensitivity, space, restraint. Rock tells you to hit. Jazz asks you to listen first. Some experienced jazz musicians recognized what I was doing in that room. I did not need more than that.

Meanwhile, the writing continued. I wrote Maa, a duet performed by Shahrzad and Faramarz Aslani, and Meshkee Yaa Talaayee. To and Farda already had melodies when I wrote their lyrics. Maa and Meshkee Yaa Talaayee did not. Each one was a different kind of puzzle.

Around 2011, the Persian rock band 1507 began after a bass player knocked on the door of apartment 1507 in North York looking for a Persian rock band to play with. I liked the idea. The name came later, after an hour of frustration in a band meeting trying to find one. Someone said 1507. That was it.

I had been listening to rock music since high school, when my uncle handed me a pair of headphones and a Pink Floyd cassette. So the writing came naturally—lyrics, melodies, arrangements. But a band changes authorship. You are no longer alone with what you hear. I deliberately let the live arrangements become democratic. Sometimes another musician heard something I had not. Sometimes the experiment went nowhere. That was part of the reason to have a band.

1507 played university gatherings and bars, played at the Government Club, and eventually performed at Tirgan Festival in 2015. Tirgan was the end of that era.

I never did bands again.

For me, the important part had never been simply playing the shows. It was the experience of putting musicians together, shaping arrangements, rehearsing, and learning what happened when something I had created alone had to survive in a room with other people. I lived it completely. Then I moved on.

Helping Other Artists — A Natural Next Step

After 1507, I spent years doing more work for other artists: ghost writing, mixing, mastering, production. Over time, that became hundreds of mixes and masters, with those projects eventually reaching millions of streams.

Every project brought a different challenge, a different voice, and another opportunity to understand sound and production more deeply. And, more importantly, the human side of artistic interactions.

Gradually, another question appeared.

If I continued along that path, I could spend the rest of my musical life making other artists’ work sound better. A career. A space in which I would have to sacrifice my own creativity.

Perhaps too easily.

Another Venue — The Lost Ambient

Time was passing faster than ever; I was becoming a dad. We were living in a house now, and I was completing my basement studio. For the first time, I was feeling content, and this led me to The Lost Ambient—an unexplored territory, close to where I had started with instrumental music.

This time, sound colours called me toward calmness, toward a space rather than a story. After all the stories I had gone through—from life to music productions—maybe it was time to settle down in a meditative realm and simply reflect for a while.

My work in clinical informatics pharmacy and later as an informatics specialist was already filling most of my time, so this was not going to be a conventional album project.

The Lost Ambient developed over roughly nine months. I completed it before my child was born in 2019. It contained nine pieces.

It was slowly bringing me back to my own space in art.

Then I began testing the waters again—carefully bringing some of my own creative ideas back into the world and seeing where they might lead.

Testing the Waters

After The Lost Ambient, I was out there one more time, looking for a team of collaborators. I hadn’t been in touch with other artists for a long while, only responding to commissioned work and referrals once in a while. I was away from the “scene” and had never really built up the kind of momentum a musician would normally have around their work. I was isolated by choice.

I talked to many artists in different countries online and attended some gatherings among the business insiders on Clubhouse. Something had changed. Maybe it was me. I wasn’t sure enough to call it.

The groups seemed divided, conversations were often argumentative and, in many cases, not particularly constructive. Things felt more business-like. The friendship and trust I remembered from when I was in Iran either didn’t exist anymore or had been pushed to the margins.

I didn’t give up. A few tracks came out of that search:

Deep End Chill, Lachin, Contrasting Enigma, and Sunset House Fusion opened another instrumental language. I liked it, but I would miss writing lyrics.

Jaaddeh, Bogzaar Bogzaram, Jaadoo, Vaghtee Khaabee, Dotaa Vaazheh, and later Har Chee Hast brought music and lyrics together again, but questions around collaboration made me think more carefully about my boundaries. These projects were all different, and they were part of the sum of my exploration—simply who and what was available to me at the time to shake hands and shape our creativity. What I learned from them was remarkably consistent.

The labour-of-love collaborations with seasoned musicians that worked best for me were built on informed mutual trust in each other’s work. If, gradually, a project became something one person wanted to make their own, those collaborations usually stopped there for me. When that balance was there, something good could happen. When it wasn’t, there was usually no reason to force it.

If it rolls, it rolls.

When the Machine Arrived — Is Artificial Bad?

After I did Tolooe To, a song from the 1507 era with Rastin—a very pleasant collaboration—another question appeared in 2024.

I began wondering whether AI, newly arrived, could fill some of the gaps in my workflow. Could it fill in for a vocalist? What about session players? Could it give me another way to hear an idea that might otherwise remain unfinished?

I first tried training AI with my own vocals. It was not good enough. I could hear that the singing techniques were there, but the result was still far below what I could get from an established singer.

Then came Udio, ElevenLabs and Suno, sometimes together.

An AI singer could technically sing better than an amateur singer in some respects. But the overall studio quality was not there. There were problems with vocal character, emotion, Persian phrasing and pronunciation, artifacts, and the unmistakable sense of artificiality.

Some had more convincing expression in Persian. Particularly then.

I released Naneveshteh, Doori and Marz in 2024 using AI singers. My first experiments with AI. These releases were labelled as research projects. I used AI to turn my melodies, harmonies and lyrics into songs with heavy studio edits that came after each generative AI work. I wanted AI music in my timeline because it was new and worth exploring in my mind, despite the criticism surrounding it. I was not trying to make a statement about its impact, nor was I trying to anticipate what it might mean for anyone else. I was simply curious, and I shared the results.

At the same time, I thought it was too early.

So I stopped and waited.

With newer versions of generative AI in 2025, I felt that, with my studio work added, I could push the vocal toward perhaps sixty to seventy percent of what I would expect from a rock singer’s studio recording after all the tricks. Maybe I could get by with non-critical ears.

Still not enough.

But enough to keep going. I had to try again.

So in mid-2025 came Saayehaa, right in my music lab, the first Persian AI rock band.

Maybe they could sing some of the 1507 songs, or some of the many songs I had written years ago and stored on my portable drives that might otherwise never get heard.

And they did.

Saayehaa

I thought it would be more interesting to play the game of creating different vocal characters—different voices, different personalities, different colours—while keeping the music mine.

Saayehaa gave me a way to do that. They were my AI version of 1507.

I could create a singer without having to find one. I could decide what that singer sounded like, how that voice behaved, where it sat in the music, and what kind of personality it seemed to have. For the first time, I could build the vocal character around the music rather than build the music around the available singer.

That mattered to me.

At the time, I couldn’t have imagined that Raha, one of Saayehaa’s AI characters, would soon be singing a duet with Rastin.

The technology was part of the palette. It was not the artistic identity.

I could still say that Saayehaa sang the song or played the guitars. I chose rock because it already has a lot of distortion as part of its sonic character. Artificiality could live inside that sound more naturally. In its own way, it was already close to what AI-generated music had to offer. I would write the lyrics and melodies, and jointly arrange the pieces with the band.

The project grew beyond an experiment. There were commercial releases, blind listening research, and eventually the possibility of revisiting some of the music I had made years earlier. I avoided Spotify for the non-human singers because I thought the rules might change soon, and they did later. At that point, Navahang was my only choice. Some of the 1507 songs came back into the picture.

Music that had originally been created for a real band could now be heard through a completely different kind of band.

By then, I had stopped being particularly interested in the argument over whether AI was “real” music. I was more interested in what it could become and how I could use it to express the feelings wrapped inside my lyrics and melodies.

I expect the technology will eventually remove some of the production pain that has slowed me down for years and shorten the distance between feeling something and having people feel it.

I don’t mind being the person behind the music. Actually, that is who I am.

To me, words are tools. Sounds are tools. AI is another tool. So are instruments, and a singer’s voice.

Underneath all the work is something much harder to define: a feeling that needs expression, and an expression that needs to trigger another feeling in someone else. Maybe even something that reminds us who we are away from all the noise.

That is the part that has remained unchanged.

Saayehaa was heard widely through Navahang, one of the places where listeners inside Iran had a chance to hear it.

AI Shaking Hands with a Human

The next logical question was whether an artificial voice and a human voice could inhabit the same Persian song convincingly with the available technology.

The idea arrived while I was deeply inside the creative process. I was writing Omid for Saayehaa when I thought: what if this could be a duet—an AI singer and a human? I did not stop to decide whether it was sensible. I just did it as soon as Rastin agreed.

The mix was challenging. I had to work with Rastin’s voice to make it live beside Raha’s. His feeling remained dominant, while Raha’s higher-register hits became her particular highlights.

The point was not simply to have a human singer carry the emotion while the artificial voice filled the technical gaps. I wanted to see whether the two could actually belong to the same musical world.

Could a human voice and an artificial one share the same song without one making the other feel out of place?

That was the experiment.

The Butterfly

Ney. Arrangement. Songwriting. Lyrics. Production. Mixing. Mastering. Drums. Bands. Ambient music. Collaboration. AI. Saayehaa, and later bringing classical Persian poems into English lyrical form using crooner-style music.

From the inside, my musical journey felt coherent, like surfing a series of waves on a sunny afternoon.

Close to the water, where I am standing, there is an ordinary butterfly going from flower to flower.

Nobody sees it. Nobody photographs it. Nobody cares enough to question its motives. Nobody knows how many flowers it has visited.

I am not sure if the butterfly needs any observers. It moves toward what attracts it. It experiences what is there. It moves on. Perhaps it contributes something it will never know about. There is a lightness there, life, a non-sticky, childlike curiosity.

Much like art has danced through my life, unconcerned, careless, free.

The sun is almost down. My ride is here.

About

The work, first.

Kambiz Mirzaei has spent decades navigating the full cycle of music-making, from instrumental composition and lyric writing to melody, arrangement, recording, mixing, mastering, and production. His work has brought him into collaboration with artists including Jamshid Andalibi, Faramarz Aslani, Shahrzad Sepanlou, and Rastin, and he continues to collaborate with seasoned musicians and artists around the globe.

He also teaches music production, sharing the practical and creative experience developed through years of independent studio work. During all these years, what has not changed for him is: the work must be at the centre, the feeling it conveys. Everything else is just a tool.

Alongside music, Kambiz holds a Doctor of Pharmacy degree and has worked in Clinical Pharmacy and Informatics in Canada for decades.
